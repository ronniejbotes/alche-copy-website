**Most professional small business websites cost between $3,000 and $15,000 to build, with ongoing costs of $100 to $400 per month.** DIY builders start near zero. Full-service agencies can exceed $35,000. The spread is enormous, and almost none of it is explained by how the site looks.

The more useful question is not what a website costs. It is what a website is supposed to *do*, and whether the quote in front of you is priced to do it. A $12,000 site that generates four qualified leads a month is cheap. A $1,500 site that generates none is expensive.

## The 2026 Price Tiers

| Option | Build Cost | Ongoing | What You Get |
|---|---|---|---|
| DIY builder | $0-$500 | $15-$50/mo | Template site you build yourself. Wix, Squarespace, Shopify. |
| Template + freelancer | $1,500-$4,000 | $50-$150/mo | Customised template, your branding, basic setup |
| Custom freelancer | $4,000-$8,000 | $100-$250/mo | Custom design, proper structure, some strategy |
| Agency build | $5,000-$20,000 | $150-$400/mo | Strategy, custom design and build, SEO foundations, conversion focus |
| Full-service / complex | $20,000-$35,000+ | $400+/mo | Multi-location, e-commerce, integrations, custom functionality |

Core recurring components are relatively fixed regardless of tier: a domain at $10-$20 a year, hosting from $2 to $120 a month, SSL at $0-$75 a year, plus whatever plugins or platform fees your build requires.

The honest summary is that most small service businesses are correctly served somewhere between $4,000 and $12,000, and the ones who pay more usually have a genuine reason — multiple locations, booking integrations, or a market where the website is the primary sales asset.

## What Actually Drives the Price

Five things, in rough order of impact.

**1. Page count and content depth.** A five-page brochure site and a forty-page site with location pages and service-area content are different projects. If you serve twelve towns and want to rank in each, you need twelve pages, and someone has to write them.

**2. Whether content is included.** This is the most common gap between a cheap quote and an expensive one. Many quotes assume *you* supply all copy and images. If you have never written a service page, this will stall your project for months. Copywriting for a small site typically runs $1,500-$5,000 on its own.

**3. Custom design versus template.** A customised template is faster and cheaper and is completely fine for most service businesses. Bespoke design costs more and matters most when your brand is a differentiator.

**4. Functionality.** Contact forms are trivial. Online booking, payment processing, customer portals, CRM integrations and multi-location logic are not. Each integration adds cost and ongoing maintenance.

**5. Whether SEO is built in or bolted on.** A site built with clean URL structure, server-rendered content, proper heading hierarchy, fast Core Web Vitals and schema markup costs more upfront and saves a great deal later. Retrofitting SEO onto a site that ignored it is frequently more expensive than building correctly the first time.

## The Costs Quotes Leave Out

Ask about every one of these before signing. They are where "budget" websites become expensive.

- **Copywriting.** Often excluded. Assume $1,500-$5,000 if you need it.
- **Photography.** Stock photos of models in hard hats do not sell trade services. Real photography runs $500-$2,500.
- **Hosting and maintenance.** $100-$400/month for managed hosting, updates, backups and security. Unmaintained sites get hacked, and a hacked site can be delisted from Google.
- **Content migration.** Moving existing blog posts and pages is manual work.
- **Redirect mapping.** Critical and frequently skipped. See the warning below.
- **Tracking setup.** Analytics, Search Console, conversion tracking, call tracking. Without these you cannot tell whether the site works.
- **Revisions beyond the included rounds.** Check how many are included and what a further round costs.

## The Redesign Mistake That Costs Most

If you are replacing an existing website, this is the single most important paragraph here.

**When URLs change and old ones are not 301-redirected to their new equivalents, you lose every ranking attached to those URLs.** Businesses routinely spend $10,000 on a beautiful new site and watch organic traffic fall by half the following month, then spend a year recovering ground they already owned.

Before any redesign, insist on: a full crawl of the existing site, a URL-by-URL redirect map, and verification in Search Console after launch. Ask any prospective agency how they handle redirects during a migration. If the answer is vague, that is disqualifying — it is a routine question with a routine answer, and not knowing it tells you they have caused this before.

The same applies to the other silent killer: **client-side JavaScript rendering**. If your new site renders content only after JavaScript runs, Google may index empty pages. Sites built this way can be genuinely invisible in search while looking perfect to you. We cover how to test for it in [why your website is not showing up on Google](/blog/why-is-my-website-not-showing-up-on-google).

## Judge a Website by Leads, Not Looks

Here is the reframe that makes the price question answerable.

Your website has one job: turn visitors into enquiries. Everything else — the animation, the palette, the photography — matters only insofar as it serves that. A site is worth what the leads it produces are worth.

Run the maths:

1. **What is a customer worth?** Average sale × repeat purchases × gross margin.
2. **What does your site convert at?** Service business sites typically convert 2-5% of relevant traffic into enquiries. Under 1% means something is broken.
3. **What is the difference worth?** If you get 1,000 visits a month and lift conversion from 1% to 3%, that is 20 extra enquiries a month. At a 25% close rate and $3,000 gross profit per customer, that is $15,000 a month.

Against that, the difference between a $4,000 site and a $10,000 site is recovered in weeks — *if* the more expensive one actually converts better. That conditional is the whole game, and it is why you should ask any agency what their sites convert at and how they know.

## What a Website Should Include at Any Serious Price

If you are paying $4,000 or more, these are not upgrades. They are the baseline.

**Fast on mobile.** Most service business traffic is mobile, often on poor connections. Core Web Vitals in the green.

**Obvious contact paths.** Phone number in the header, tappable on mobile. Forms above the fold on service pages. A clear primary action on every page.

**A page per service.** One page listing eight services ranks for nothing. Eight pages each covering one service properly can rank for eight things.

**A page per service area**, if you serve multiple towns — with genuinely different content, not the same paragraph with the town name swapped.

**Proof.** Reviews, case studies, real photos of real work, licence and insurance details, guarantees.

**Clean technical foundation.** Server-rendered content, logical URLs, proper headings, XML sitemap, schema markup.

**Tracking from day one.** Analytics, Search Console, and conversion tracking on every form and phone click.

**You own everything.** Domain, hosting, code and accounts registered in your name. If an agency holds your domain, leaving costs you the site.

## DIY, Freelancer or Agency?

**DIY makes sense** when you are pre-revenue or testing an idea. A Squarespace site with a good Google Business Profile is a perfectly reasonable first step and beats no website enormously. The cost is your time and a ceiling on SEO control.

**A freelancer makes sense** for most established single-location service businesses. $4,000-$8,000 buys a genuinely good site. The risk is bus factor — one person who may be unavailable when something breaks. Ask what happens after launch.

**An agency makes sense** when the website is a primary revenue channel, when you have multiple locations or services, or when you want [the site built as part of a broader lead generation strategy](/web-design) rather than in isolation. You are paying for strategy and continuity, not just build hours.

The genuinely wrong answer is a $500 website for a business that depends on inbound leads. It will be slow, unrankable, and will need replacing within eighteen months.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do I need a website if I have a Google Business Profile and Facebook page?**
You can get leads without one, and some trades do. But your Google Business Profile ranks better when it links to a real website, you cannot rank organically without one, AI search engines cite websites rather than social profiles, and you do not own social platforms. It is a ceiling on growth, not an absolute barrier.

**Is WordPress still the right choice?**
For most small businesses, yes — it is flexible, SEO-capable and widely supported. Squarespace and Webflow are legitimate alternatives with less maintenance overhead. The platform matters far less than whether the build is fast, crawlable and structured for conversion.

**How long does a website take to build?**
Four to twelve weeks for a typical small business site. The bottleneck is almost always content, not development. Projects that stall are usually waiting on copy and photos from the client.

**Should I rebuild or improve what I have?**
If your current site is indexed, reasonably fast and structurally sound, improving it is usually cheaper and safer — you keep your rankings. Rebuild when the site is unfixably slow, cannot be edited, is not mobile-friendly, or was built in a way search engines cannot read.

**How much should I budget per month after launch?**
$100-$400 covers managed hosting, updates, backups, security and small changes. Budget separately for [SEO](/seo) and [ads](/ads) — a website is a destination, not a traffic source. Building a site and expecting visitors is the most common misunderstanding we see.
