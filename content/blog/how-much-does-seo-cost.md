**Most small businesses pay between $750 and $5,000 per month for SEO in 2026.** Local campaigns sit at the bottom of that range, national and e-commerce campaigns at the top, and the majority of legitimate full-service work lands between $1,500 and $3,000 per month.

That range is wide enough to be almost useless on its own, which is the problem with most pricing articles. The number that matters is not the market average — it is what you should pay, given your market, your margins and what a customer is worth to you. This is how to work that out.

## What the 2026 Market Data Actually Says

Two industry surveys give the clearest picture available.

An Ahrefs poll of 439 SEO professionals put the average agency retainer at **$3,209 per month**, with the single most common range falling between $501 and $2,000. The average is dragged upward by enterprise contracts; the mode is closer to what a small business actually encounters.

A GoodFirms survey of 300+ agencies across 20+ countries found **48% operate in the $1,500-$5,000 monthly tier** and **43.3% charge under $1,500**. So roughly nine in ten agencies price below $5,000 a month.

One more data point worth knowing: **56.2% of agencies raised their rates going into 2026**, and the reason they gave was the added workload of optimising for AI search visibility alongside traditional rankings. You are now paying for two scoreboards instead of one.

These are practitioner survey figures, not audited financials. Treat them as a map of the market, not a price list.

## What Each Price Tier Actually Buys

Price tiers are not really about quality. They are about *how much work per month* and *how competitive the target market is*. Here is what the money buys in practice.

| Monthly Spend | Typical Scope | Realistic Fit |
|---|---|---|
| Under $750 | Google Business Profile management, basic citations, occasional content | Single-location business in a low-competition town |
| $750-$1,500 | Local SEO, GBP optimisation, 2-4 pages of content, review generation, basic technical | Service business targeting one city with moderate competition |
| $1,500-$3,000 | Full local + organic: technical SEO, 4-8 content pieces, link acquisition, AI search optimisation, multi-location pages | Most established service businesses. The mainstream tier. |
| $3,000-$5,000 | Competitive markets, multi-location or multi-service, aggressive content velocity, digital PR | Legal, medical, finance, home services in major metros |
| $5,000+ | National, e-commerce, or displacing well-funded incumbents | Businesses where a customer is worth thousands and competitors spend heavily |

One-time project work — a technical cleanup, a migration, a one-off content build — typically runs **$1,000 to $5,000**.

### Why the same service costs $900 in one market and $4,000 in another

Because SEO pricing is not cost-plus, it is competition-plus. The work required to rank for "plumber Boerne TX" and "personal injury lawyer Dallas" is not the same work at a different price. It is a different quantity of work entirely — more content, more links, more time, against opponents who have been investing for years.

Ask any agency quoting you a price: *what does the competitive landscape look like for my target terms, and how did that shape this number?* If they cannot answer, the price was pulled from a package sheet rather than your market.

## Pricing Models, and Which to Avoid

**Monthly retainer.** The standard. You pay a fixed fee for an agreed scope of work. Predictable for both sides, and the right default for ongoing SEO. The risk is paying for activity rather than outcomes — which is why the deliverables need to be specific in writing.

**Project-based.** A defined piece of work with a defined end. Good for technical audits, site migrations, or a one-time content build. Bad as a substitute for ongoing SEO, because rankings decay when work stops.

**Hourly.** Typically $75-$200/hour. Fine for consulting or advisory work. Poor for execution, because it rewards slow work and makes budgeting impossible.

**Pay-for-performance / pay-per-ranking.** Avoid. The incentive is to rank you for easy keywords nobody searches, so the agency can invoice for "results" that produce no leads. It also creates pressure toward tactics that risk penalties, because the agency carries the downside only until they get paid.

**Anything under about $500/month.** Be careful. Real SEO involves technical work, content production and outreach — all labour. Below roughly $500 the numbers only work if the agency is spreading one junior across forty accounts, or automating output that Google is actively good at detecting. Cheap SEO is not slow SEO. It is frequently negative SEO, and cleaning up after it costs more than doing it properly the first time.

## What Should Be Included at Any Serious Price Point

If you are paying $1,500+ per month, these should all be in scope. If any are missing, ask why.

**Technical SEO** — crawlability, indexation, site speed and Core Web Vitals, mobile usability, structured data, redirect and canonical hygiene. Without this, nothing else works.

**Keyword and intent research** — mapped to specific pages, with commercial intent identified. Not a list of 500 keywords in a spreadsheet.

**On-page optimisation** — titles, meta descriptions, heading structure, internal linking, content depth on your money pages.

**Content production** — an agreed number of pieces per month, written for your buyers, not spun for volume.

**Link acquisition** — real outreach, digital PR, partnerships. Not directory blasts or private blog networks.

**Local SEO** — Google Business Profile management, citation consistency, review generation, location pages, if you serve a geographic area.

**AI search optimisation** — schema markup, entity signals, FAQ structuring, E-E-A-T. This is the newest line item and increasingly the one that separates a 2026 service from a 2019 one. Our [AI SEO guide](/blog/what-is-ai-seo) explains what it involves.

**Reporting that shows leads, not rankings** — traffic and position data are inputs. Calls, forms and revenue are outputs. You should be shown both.

## Work Out What *You* Should Pay

Market averages do not tell you your number. This does.

**Step 1: What is a customer worth?**
Average sale value × how many times they buy × your gross margin. A roofer at $12,000 per job with 35% margin has a customer worth roughly $4,200 in gross profit.

**Step 2: What is your close rate on inbound leads?**
Be honest. Most service businesses close 15-30% of genuinely inbound, high-intent leads.

**Step 3: What can you afford per lead?**
If a customer produces $4,200 in gross profit and you close 25%, each lead is worth about $1,050 in expected gross profit. Paying $200 per lead is comfortable. Paying $900 is not.

**Step 4: How many leads would make the retainer worth it?**
At a $2,500/month retainer, you need roughly three leads a month to break even at $1,050 expected value each — and that is before accounting for the fact that organic leads keep arriving after you stop paying.

Run this before you talk to any agency. It converts "is $2,500 a lot?" into "how many leads does $2,500 need to produce?", which is an answerable question. Our post on [what a lead is actually worth](/blog/how-much-are-leads-worth) walks through the formula in more detail, and [CPL benchmarks by industry](/blog/cost-per-lead-benchmarks) gives you realistic comparison figures.

## In-House vs Agency vs Freelancer

**Freelancer: $500-$2,000/month.** One person, usually strong in one discipline. Good value if their strength matches your bottleneck. The risk is that SEO needs technical, content and outreach skills simultaneously, and almost nobody is genuinely good at all three.

**Agency: $1,500-$5,000/month.** A team covering all disciplines, with process and tooling already paid for. The risk is being a small account inside a large agency and receiving junior attention.

**In-house: $70,000-$120,000/year, plus tools.** A competent SEO manager costs more than almost any agency retainer once you add salary, benefits, and $300-$500/month in software. It makes sense above roughly $8,000-$10,000/month of equivalent agency spend, or when SEO is core to the business model.

For most service businesses doing under $10M in revenue, an agency or a strong freelancer is the correct answer on cost alone.

## Red Flags in an SEO Quote

- **Guaranteed rankings.** Nobody controls Google's algorithm. A guarantee is either meaningless or attached to keywords nobody searches.
- **No discovery before a price.** A number quoted before anyone has looked at your site or your competitors is a package, not a proposal.
- **Vague deliverables.** "Ongoing optimisation" and "content as needed" are not scope. Get counts and categories in writing.
- **Long lock-ins with no exit.** Twelve-month contracts are common and not inherently wrong, but there should be a performance-based exit.
- **You do not own the assets.** Your website, content, Search Console, Analytics and Google Business Profile must be in your accounts. If an agency owns them, leaving costs you everything you paid for.
- **Rankings-only reporting.** If the report never mentions leads, they are measuring what is easy rather than what matters.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is cheap SEO worth trying first?**
Usually not. Sub-$500 SEO tends to produce either nothing or active damage — spam links and thin AI-spun content that require a cleanup project later. If budget is genuinely tight, spend it on Google Business Profile optimisation and review generation, which are high-leverage and largely a time cost.

**Should SEO cost more than Google Ads?**
They are different shapes. Ads cost scales with volume and stops when you stop paying. SEO cost is roughly flat while results compound. Most businesses under $5,000/month total budget do better splitting across both — see [SEO or Google Ads](/blog/seo-vs-google-ads) for how to divide it.

**How long before SEO spend pays back?**
Typically six to fifteen months, depending on customer value and competition. Our [SEO timeline breakdown](/blog/how-long-does-seo-take) covers the month-by-month view and the leading indicators to watch before revenue arrives.

**Do I need to keep paying forever?**
No, but rankings decay when work stops — usually over three to six months as competitors publish and content ages. Many businesses scale down to a maintenance retainer after 12-18 months rather than stopping outright.

**Why did my quote go up compared to last year?**
Over half of agencies raised rates for 2026, mainly because AI search optimisation added work without replacing any. You are now being optimised for Google rankings *and* for citation inside AI answers.
